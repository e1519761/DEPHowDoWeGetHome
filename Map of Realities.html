    02 / WHAT WE FOUND

    The patterns behind
    the walk.

    Each corner of the pentagon represents a sense (see, smell, feel, touch and hear), and the words clustered around it are the cues residents named when describing how they know they are nearly home. Together they show that wayfinding goes well beyond signs and arrows: people build rich mental maps from cooking smells, playground colours, the echo of a lift lobby or the change of ground underfoot.

    Word size reflects how many responses mentioned it, so the words that stand out point to shared experiences: cues that many different people independently rely on to orient themselves. As the study moves beyond this pilot to more locations, the same diagram can be recreated for each one, allowing comparisons between areas. Some places may trigger certain senses more strongly than others, and where a sense is barely used at all, there is an opportunity gap. Those gaps can inform design decisions that make a space richer in experience as well as more accessible.

    03 / WHO WALKS DIFFERENTLY

    Wayfinding varies
    for everyone.

    The same estate reads differently depending on who is walking it.

    01

    Families with children

    The playground becomes an important signal of home, while the sidewalk and main road form a strict boundary that marks leaving it.

    02

    Seniors

    Elevated walkways, stairs and moving between several levels are the hardest parts of the walk.

    03

    At night

    Compact void decks are confusing because few visual elements stand out, and after dark the area becomes quiet and dim.

    04

    Children and cyclists

    Their boundaries are wider. They find their way from the bike lanes and from large cues, like HDB blocks or condos, that tell them they are in the right neighbourhood.

    Every shared journey helps us understand how neighbourhoods could become easier to navigate.
